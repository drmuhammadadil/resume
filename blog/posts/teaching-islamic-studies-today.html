Academic article by Dr. Muhammad Adil

Teaching Islamic Studies in Contemporary Universities

Published: 2026-09-26 · Author: Dr. Muhammad Adil

Teaching Islamic Studies in a contemporary university requires more than the transmission of information. It involves helping students understand foundational texts, classical scholarly traditions and historical contexts while also developing the analytical skills needed to engage modern academic questions.

From memorization to structured understanding

Students need a reliable foundation in Qur’anic studies, Hadith, Islamic jurisprudence, Seerah and intellectual history. Yet a university course should also show how these areas relate to one another. A legal ruling has a history of interpretation; a historical narrative has source questions; a theological concept may have different formulations across schools and periods.

Source literacy is essential

One of the most useful skills an Islamic Studies program can develop is source literacy. Students should learn to distinguish primary from secondary sources, recognize different genres of classical writing and understand how later scholars cite, interpret and sometimes disagree with earlier authorities.

This is particularly important when students encounter quotations through social media or search engines. Academic training should help them ask where a text comes from, what its original wording is, what its historical context was and how relevant scholars have understood it.

Connecting classical disciplines with contemporary questions

Contemporary university students often want to understand how Islamic intellectual traditions engage questions of law, ethics, conflict, peacebuilding, citizenship, technology and social change. These subjects can enrich the curriculum when they are introduced through well-defined research questions rather than broad claims.

For example, a course on siyar may include comparison with international humanitarian law, while a Seerah course may examine diplomacy, treaties or reconciliation. Such comparisons are most useful when students are taught to identify both similarities and differences instead of assuming that two systems are identical.

Research and writing skills

Academic writing should be treated as part of subject learning. Students benefit from practice in formulating research questions, organizing literature reviews, citing sources consistently and distinguishing evidence from interpretation.

  • Define a precise research question before collecting material.
  • Use primary sources where appropriate and identify reliable editions.
  • Engage relevant modern scholarship rather than relying only on summaries.
  • State clearly when a point is contested or when the evidence is limited.
  • Use citations consistently so that readers can verify the evidence.

A balanced academic environment

The strongest Islamic Studies classrooms combine intellectual seriousness with respect for the religious significance of the material being studied. Critical inquiry does not require dismissiveness, and reverence does not require the avoidance of difficult questions.

Universities can therefore play an important role in preparing a new generation of researchers who are grounded in the Islamic scholarly tradition and capable of participating in wider academic conversations.
